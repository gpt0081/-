## Key findings
1. Rhee's July 9 written position accepted non-obstruction of the armistice, but with a national-survival qualification. Robertson treated this as a major retreat from earlier demands, not an unconditional guarantee.
2. The Korean July 9 treaty draft was materially broader than the U.S. position: it asserted ROK jurisdiction through the Yalu/Tumen and used stronger immediate-assistance language.
3. UNC separated command of ROK military forces from the ability to guarantee every act of the ROK government. Clark explicitly avoided promising force against ROKA merely to enforce governmental compliance.
4. Robertson later stated that he avoided taking over POW questions because they fell within Clark's UNC authority. This helps separate diplomatic bargaining from military-command responsibility.
5. The reconstruction file is not a simple aid-total story. It is an institutional-governance story involving FOA, CINCUNC/Economic Coordinator, UNKRA, CEB, congressional appropriations, military conversion rates, local-cost burdens, and sequencing with ROK force expansion.
6. Paik Tu-chin was a substantive actor, not merely a presidential attendee: he pressed CEB implementation, exchange/conversion-rate settlement, local expenditure burdens, Tasca-program commitment and aid duration.
7. On July 27 Rhee told Clark he would announce cooperation with the armistice, while the ROK remained outside the signature itself.
