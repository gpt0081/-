## Why this pass
Pass 57 followed the release into Korean administrative afterlife. Pass 58 returns to the contemporaneous decision sequence and reads a continuous run of FRUS documents rather than isolated quotations. The aim is to separate (a) Rhee's own order, (b) military-police execution and competing ROKA command channels, (c) provincial/police welfare instructions, (d) ROK diplomatic advocacy, and (e) U.S./UNC reactions and bargaining.

## Findings with attribution boundaries

### 1. Direct presidential order is unusually well evidenced
FRUS d607 reproduces Rhee's June 18 letter to Mark Clark. Rhee says he took responsibility and ordered Korean military police to release the prisoners. The enclosed ROK Office of Public Information release likewise says the release was ordered on his own responsibility. This is direct actor testimony, not merely later attribution.

At the same time, d607 also says provincial governors and police officers were instructed to care for released prisoners. This supports a separate post-release civilian-administration action. It does not identify the individual governors or prove how uniformly the instruction was executed.

### 2. Operational command was not simply `President → whole ROK Army`
FRUS d612 records Clark reporting that Provost Marshal General Won Yong-dok had issued orders to ROK security units near camps to attack U.S. forces preventing release, while ROK Army Chief of Staff Paik Sun-yup had been informed of UNC intentions and, in Clark's account, understood them. Clark further characterized Won as acting directly under Rhee and issuing a separate instruction channel. This is strong contemporaneous evidence for a split command model and a reason to avoid attributing every ROKA action to the regular Army chain.

### 3. The crisis persisted after the first mass release
FRUS d613–d621 show continuing concern over additional breakouts, U.S. troop substitution for disaffected ROK guards, and the possibility of U.S.–ROK armed clashes. d617 reports U.S. infantry companies controlling eight remaining camps while nearby ROK security battalions outnumbered them. d620 records Taylor's June 21 meeting with Rhee concerning no further unilateral release of Chinese POWs and avoiding conflict at Korean POW camps.

### 4. Pyun Yung-tai was an independent diplomatic actor, not merely a presidential echo
FRUS d608 identifies Foreign Minister Pyun's June 18 letter to Clark setting out the South Korean position. FRUS d616 records Ambassador Briggs meeting Rhee on June 19 with Acting Prime Minister Pyun present. This strengthens Pyun's node across foreign-minister, acting-prime-minister and crisis-diplomacy roles. His exact drafting/decision role in the release order remains separate and unproven by these documents.

### 5. Paek Tu-chin and Yang You-chan were crisis diplomats in Washington
FRUS d611 records Prime Minister Paek Tu-chin and Ambassador Yang You-chan meeting Dulles, Robertson, Frank Nash, Kenneth Young and Charles Sullivan on June 18 specifically over the unilateral POW release. This is contemporaneous evidence for their participation in managing the diplomatic consequences. It is not evidence that either helped plan the camp operation.

### 6. The U.S. response rapidly became a multi-track bargain
FRUS d626 records Dulles's instructions for Robertson around three tracks: mutual-defense treaty, economic assistance tied to the Tasca recommendations, and the political conference/unification question. FRUS d630 reproduces Rhee's June 22 draft aide-mémoire, which linked armistice cooperation to security, industrialization, defense strength and political-conference conditions. The crisis therefore should be modeled as a sequence connecting POW release, alliance-command authority, treaty bargaining, economic reconstruction and post-armistice political strategy.

### 7. Economic planning is not a side note
FRUS d632 (NSC 156, June 23) formally transmits the Tasca mission report under the title `Strengthening the Korean Economy`. It belongs in the same decision window as the armistice confrontation. This reinforces earlier passes that treated reconstruction/economic planning as a first-class Rhee-era policy domain rather than background context.

### 8. UNC authority and ROK consent were distinct legal-political questions
FRUS d618 records Clark stating that, in the immediate situation, the UNC could not honestly answer that it controlled the South Korean government and army or that Rhee's government was necessarily included in an armistice. d636 later authorized Clark wide latitude to negotiate while avoiding an obligation to use force against the ROK to compel compliance. This is important for claim modeling: UNC authority, ROK governmental consent, ROKA operational control, and actual compliance are separate propositions.

## Adversarial checks performed
1. Tested `all ROKA followed a single release order`: contradicted by evidence of Paik vs Won command-channel distinction.
2. Tested `release ended operational crisis on June 18`: contradicted by d613–d621 continued breakout/clash preparations.
3. Tested `Pyun planned the release`: corpus shows advocacy/presence, not operational planning; remains insufficient.
4. Tested `Paek planned the release`: d611 proves diplomatic response, not planning; remains insufficient.
5. Tested `Yang planned the release`: same; remains insufficient.
6. Tested `Rhee hid his own responsibility afterward`: d607 explicitly claims responsibility; contradicted.
7. Tested `Rhee informed Clark in advance`: d607 explicitly says he withheld the plan; supports non-notification.
8. Tested `all camp guards were under normal ROKA command`: d612/d617 indicate provost-marshal/security-unit channel requiring finer mapping.
9. Tested `the dispute was only about POWs`: d626/d630/d632 show defense treaty, economic aid, industrialization and political conference linkage.
10. Tested `UNC could straightforwardly bind ROK compliance`: d618/d636 show contemporaneous uncertainty and coercion limits.
