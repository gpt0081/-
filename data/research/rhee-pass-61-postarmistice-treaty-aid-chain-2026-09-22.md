# Rhee Pass 61 — Post-armistice treaty, aid, and command chain

Date: 2026-09-22
Scope: 1953-08-05 through 1953-11-04, with July 9 ROK treaty draft as baseline.
Source family directly opened this pass: U.S. Department of State, FRUS 1952–1954, Korea XV pt.2, docs 682, 734, 735, 737, 738, 739, 740, 741, 762, 763, 772, 774, 792. Search discovery also checked surrounding volume/index material. These are U.S. official diplomatic/intelligence records; they do not substitute for Korean-origin cabinet, Foreign Ministry, National Assembly, or military files.

## Main finding
The mutual-defense story must be modeled as a sequence of different legal/political objects, not as one continuous promise. The July 9 ROK draft sought unusually broad commitments: recognition of ROK jurisdiction north to the Yalu/Tumen and immediate assistance after an armed attack. During the August Dulles consultations, Rhee continued to press for stronger/automatic action while a bilateral working party and the Korean cabinet-level group negotiated text. By August 7 the U.S. draft was accepted with minor textual changes after the automatic-action argument failed; on August 8 Foreign Minister Pyun and Secretary Dulles initialed the draft. Pyun then came to Washington and signed the treaty October 1. The treaty nevertheless remained entangled with a separate armistice-compliance bargain: Eisenhower’s November 4 letter explicitly recalled Rhee’s August 8 agreement to keep ROK forces under UNC while the treaty moved toward entry into force, and warned that unilateral war could imperil Senate ratification.

This means `ROK July draft`, `Aug. working-party text`, `Aug. 8 initialed draft`, `Oct. 1 signed treaty`, and `armistice/UNC assurance` must remain separate document/event nodes.

## Adversarial checks
1. Claim: July 9 ROK draft was basically the final treaty. Countercheck: its territorial-jurisdiction and immediate-action language is materially broader than the later negotiated framework.
2. Claim: Eisenhower promised a treaty stronger than the Philippines treaty. Countercheck: doc 739 records Dulles rejecting Paek’s interpretation and the memorandum calling it a genuine misconstruction.
3. Claim: Rhee secured automatic U.S. war entry. Countercheck: docs 739–740 show repeated rejection of automatic action.
4. Claim: Aug. 8 was treaty signature. Countercheck: doc 741 calls it initialing; doc 762 notes Pyun arrived in Washington to sign Oct. 1.
5. Claim: treaty alone guaranteed armistice compliance. Countercheck: Nov. 4 Eisenhower letter treats the Aug. 8 UNC/armistice arrangement as a distinct assurance linked politically to ratification.
6. Claim: Pyun’s Oct. 2 demilitarization remarks were official ROK policy. Countercheck: he expressly asked that they be treated as personal and non-committal.
7. Claim: post-armistice talks were only security diplomacy. Countercheck: docs 737–738 contain substantial aid governance, industrial self-sufficiency, exchange-rate, local-cost, and force-development business.
8. Claim: Paek was merely ceremonial. Countercheck: doc 738 shows direct economic/military bargaining and doc 739 a treaty-interpretation intervention.
9. Claim: SE-48 proves exact Rhee command orders. Countercheck: it is an intelligence estimate about capabilities/probable action, not an order log.
10. Claim: UNC operational control made unilateral ROK action impossible. Countercheck: SE-48 judged Rhee retained substantial practical leverage and described Provost Marshal channels, but this remains U.S. intelligence assessment requiring Korean military corroboration.
11. Claim: U.S. aid commitment was a single fixed billion-dollar appropriation. Countercheck: doc 737 distinguishes a $200m initial mechanism from future congressional appropriations and describes organizational details as unsettled.
12. Claim: CEB supremacy was already an agreed bilateral implementation fact on Aug. 6. Countercheck: Rhee advocated it and the July 31 ROK MOU sought U.S. concurrence; Stevens said U.S. review was still required.
