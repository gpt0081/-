## Findings

### 1. ROK participation was treated as structurally necessary, not merely desirable
Dulles d746 explicitly argued that a Korean political conference boycotted by the ROK could not succeed. He also rejected making Washington responsible for selling Rhee a great-power roundtable that the United States itself did not support. This sharpens the event graph: Rhee/ROK had bargaining leverage through participation/nonparticipation, but that is not the same as a formal legal veto.

### 2. The conference-composition dispute became a multi-actor network
The Swedish-Peiping report in d747 attributed to Zhou Enlai an 11-power roundtable concept: US, USSR, UK, France, PRC, India, DPRK, ROK, Poland, Burma, Sweden, with binding decisions requiring agreement of armistice parties. d749 shows competing UN-side designs and actors: Lodge/Lloyd compromise over Soviet participation; Australia/New Zealand proposal for India; Canada, Belgium, Netherlands pressure over force-contributor participation; India and Asian-African/Latin-American positions. This is an event family, not a bilateral Rhee-Dulles episode.

### 3. India participation was contested on several distinct grounds
In d749, Lodge warned that ROK would not participate if India were included and reserved the U.S. position if ROK stayed out; Canadian Paul Martin and Australian Percy Spender challenged the idea that Rhee should determine participant selection. d750 then records U.S. Ambassador George V. Allen asking Nehru for voluntary Indian withdrawal, while Nehru objected that Washington had tied itself too closely to ROK. These positions must be represented separately rather than collapsed into `ROK opposed India`.

### 4. UNGA Resolution 711(VII) had a staged legislative history
The d748/d752 editorial notes separate: reconvening under Resolution 705(VII); referral to First Committee; force-contributor/ROK draft; Soviet-participation draft; India proposal; committee votes; Menon's withdrawal of the India plenary vote; and final adoption on 1953-08-28 of components A, B and D as Resolution 711(VII). Event graph should preserve proposal, committee adoption/withdrawal, plenary adoption, and communication to Beijing/Pyongyang.

### 5. `Rhee had a veto` is too strong
The record shows practical leverage and U.S. fear that ROK nonparticipation could wreck the conference, but d749 also records Martin and Spender explicitly challenging any Rhee veto over participant selection. Use `participation leverage / threatened nonparticipation`, not a legal veto, unless a legal source establishes one.

### 6. `India was excluded by a U.S.-ROK veto` is also too strong
The First Committee adopted the India invitation on Aug. 27; d752 says Krishna Menon withdrew the proposal before plenary decision because he judged peace better served by not forcing it to a vote. The causal chain therefore includes committee support, Indian agency, and plenary withdrawal.

### 7. Failure contingency planning was institutionally separate from Rhee's threats
Nash d753 states Defense/JCS concern that NSC policy did not yet specify U.S. action if the political conference collapsed, especially if ROK rather than communists restarted military operations. This independently reinforces pass 69's finding that there was no simple automatic U.S. war commitment after conference failure.

### 8. ANZUS records preserve a retrospective U.S. interpretation, not a neutral fact statement
In d754 Dulles framed the POW release as a jolt, said ROK could not be ignored, and described the security treaty and reconstruction contribution as prices paid to secure cooperation. The same record says Rhee's distrust of India shaped U.S. conference tactics. These are important contemporaneous U.S. policy interpretations, but should be typed as `contemporaneous_observation/policy rationale`, not direct proof of Rhee's private motives.

### 9. The Sweden channel continued after UNGA action
Dulles d755 says the United States had sent time/place proposals for the conference to the Chinese Communists through Sweden and expected a constructive response after UN firmness. This gives the Sweden/PRC intermediary chain a concrete post-August-28 continuation.

### 10. Date anomaly in d751 must remain open
The FRUS d751 page labels the Cabinet meeting August 27, 1954 while placing it between August 1953 conference documents and describing Dulles's recent Korea trip and immediate UN debate. Do not silently normalize it to 1953. Register as a source-date anomaly lead and inspect the Eisenhower Whitman-file original/editorial apparatus.

## Adversarial checks

1. `ROK/Rhee possessed a formal veto over conference membership` → NOT ESTABLISHED; records show practical leverage, and allied actors explicitly disputed veto logic.
2. `India was simply excluded by Rhee` → CONTRADICTED/NARROWED; committee adopted invitation, Menon later withdrew plenary proposal.
3. `Political conference was bilateral U.S.-ROK planning` → CONTRADICTED; UN, UK, Commonwealth, India, Sweden, PRC, USSR and force contributors shaped composition.
4. `All UN allies accepted the U.S. two-sides model` → CONTRADICTED; UK and others pushed against perpetuating it.
5. `Soviet participation meant Soviet membership on the UN side` → NARROWED; Lodge/Lloyd compromise conditioned Soviet participation on the other side desiring it.
6. `ROK nonparticipation was legally fatal` → NOT ESTABLISHED; U.S. policy judged it practically fatal.
7. `Conference failure automatically triggered U.S. military action` → CONTRADICTED by d753's unresolved contingency planning.
8. `U.S. policy toward India was solely deference to Rhee` → NARROWED; troop risk, conference viability, Commonwealth diplomacy and U.S. two-side interpretation also appear.
9. `Zhou's 11-power proposal was delivered directly to Washington` → CONTRADICTED/NARROWED; d747 is a Swedish diplomatic report with uncertain directness of the ambassador's access to Zhou.
10. `d751 proves an August 1953 cabinet commitment exactly as dated` → BLOCKED/CONFLICTED pending source-date check because FRUS page says 1954 despite sequence/context.
